# Dinosaur Lecture — Question and Answer Material

## What a dinosaur is

Dinosaurs were a group of reptiles that lived on land. They all walked with their
legs held straight underneath their bodies, rather than sprawling out to the
sides like a crocodile or a lizard. That upright stance is one of the features
scientists use to tell a dinosaur from other ancient reptiles.

Dinosaurs first appeared about two hundred and thirty million years ago. The
last of the non-bird dinosaurs died out about sixty-six million years ago, so
they existed for roughly one hundred and sixty-five million years.

Scientists have named more than a thousand different kinds of dinosaur, and
about fifty new kinds are named every year.

## Animals that are often mistaken for dinosaurs

Several famous prehistoric animals were not dinosaurs at all.

- Pterodactyls and other pterosaurs were flying reptiles. They were close cousins
  of dinosaurs, but they were not dinosaurs.
- Plesiosaurs, ichthyosaurs and mosasaurs were swimming reptiles, not dinosaurs.
  No dinosaur lived in the sea.
- Dimetrodon, the animal with the sail on its back, lived long before the
  dinosaurs and was more closely related to mammals than to them.
- Woolly mammoths and sabre-toothed cats lived millions of years after the
  non-bird dinosaurs had already died out.

People and non-bird dinosaurs never met. The last non-bird dinosaurs died about
sixty-six million years ago, and the first humans appeared only a few hundred
thousand years ago.

Not all dinosaurs lived at the same time either. Stegosaurus died out about
eighty million years before Tyrannosaurus rex appeared. There is more time
between Stegosaurus and Tyrannosaurus rex than between Tyrannosaurus rex and us.

## The three ages of dinosaurs

The time of the dinosaurs is divided into three periods.

- The Triassic, when the first small dinosaurs appeared.
- The Jurassic, the age of the giant long-necked sauropods and Stegosaurus.
- The Cretaceous, the last and longest period, when Tyrannosaurus rex,
  Triceratops, Velociraptor and Ankylosaurus lived.

## Sauropods: the long-necked giants

Sauropods were the largest animals ever to walk on land. They all ate plants,
they all walked on four legs, and they all had long necks and long tails.

Brachiosaurus was about twenty-two metres long and weighed somewhere between
thirty and fifty tonnes, which is about as much as ten elephants. Unusually, its
front legs were longer than its back legs, so its shoulders sloped upwards and
its head was held high.

The longest neck belonged to a sauropod called Mamenchisaurus. Its neck alone was
about fifteen metres long, which is longer than most buses.

Argentinosaurus may have been the heaviest of all, at perhaps seventy tonnes.

Sauropods did not chew. They stripped leaves off branches with peg-shaped teeth
and swallowed them whole. Many of them swallowed stones as well. These stones,
called gastroliths, sat in the stomach and helped grind up the food. Birds such
as chickens do the same thing today.

A sauropod's long neck let it reach food that nothing else could reach, and it
also let the animal stand in one place and sweep its head across a wide area
without moving its huge body. Moving that much weight around costs a lot of
energy, so standing still and reaching was a very efficient way to eat.

Even the biggest sauropods hatched from eggs no bigger than a football. A baby
sauropod had to grow from a few kilograms to many tonnes, and it did so
remarkably fast, reaching adult size in about twenty to thirty years.

## Meat-eating dinosaurs

Meat-eating dinosaurs are called theropods. They walked on two legs and most had
sharp, curved teeth.

### Tyrannosaurus rex

Tyrannosaurus rex was about twelve metres long and weighed six or seven tonnes.
Its skull was roughly one and a half metres long. Its largest teeth were about
thirty centimetres from root to tip, which is about the length of a banana, and
they were thick and round rather than blade-like, built for crushing bone.

Tyrannosaurus rex had the strongest bite of any land animal known. Its arms were
short, only about one metre long, but they were extremely muscular. Scientists
are still not sure what it used them for.

It probably could not run quickly. Its best speed was likely around twenty
kilometres per hour, a fast walk or gentle jog for a person on a bicycle. It was
simply too heavy to sprint safely.

### Velociraptor

The real Velociraptor was about the size of a turkey, roughly two metres long
including its tail, and it weighed about fifteen kilograms. It was covered in
feathers. We know this because fossil arm bones have been found with quill knobs
on them, the same bumps that anchor feathers in birds today.

It had one large curved claw on the second toe of each foot, which it held raised
off the ground as it ran. The huge raptors shown in films are much closer in size
to a different dinosaur called Deinonychus.

Velociraptor fossils come from Mongolia. One famous fossil, known as the Fighting
Dinosaurs, preserves a Velociraptor and a Protoceratops locked together, buried
by a collapsing sand dune while they fought.

### Spinosaurus

Spinosaurus was even longer than Tyrannosaurus rex, with a tall sail on its back
and a long, narrow snout like a crocodile's. It ate fish and spent much of its
time in water.

## Armoured and horned dinosaurs

Plant-eating dinosaurs had many ways of defending themselves.

### Triceratops

Triceratops had three horns, one short horn on its nose and two long horns above
its eyes, which could reach a metre in length. Behind the horns was a wide frill
of solid bone around its neck. Its skull could be over two metres long, one of
the largest heads of any land animal ever.

The horns were probably used both to fight off predators and to display to other
Triceratops, in the same way that deer use antlers today. Some Triceratops skulls
have healed wounds that match the horns of another Triceratops.

### Stegosaurus

Stegosaurus was about nine metres long and had two rows of large flat plates
standing up along its back. Scientists once thought the plates were armour, but
they are too thin and too brittle for that. They were probably used for display,
and possibly to help the animal warm up or cool down.

At the end of its tail were four long spikes, which it could swing sideways as a
weapon. The spikes have a nickname, the thagomizer. A Stegosaurus brain was quite
small for such a large animal, about the size of a plum.

### Ankylosaurus

Ankylosaurus was covered in thick plates of bone set into its skin, even over its
eyelids. At the end of its tail was a heavy club of solid bone. A swing from that
club could break the leg bones of an attacking predator.

## Feathers, and why birds are dinosaurs

Many dinosaurs had feathers, especially the meat-eating theropods. The first
feathers were simple hair-like fuzz used for keeping warm and for display, long
before any dinosaur could fly.

Birds evolved from small feathered theropod dinosaurs during the Jurassic period.
Archaeopteryx, which lived about one hundred and fifty million years ago, is one
of the best-known early birds, and it still had teeth, claws on its wings and a
long bony tail.

Because birds descend from dinosaurs, scientists consider birds to be dinosaurs.
There are about eleven thousand species of bird alive today, so in that sense
dinosaurs are still one of the most successful groups of animals on Earth.

## What colour were dinosaurs?

For most dinosaurs, nobody knows. Colour is very rarely preserved.

For a few dinosaurs with fossilised feathers, scientists have found microscopic
structures called melanosomes, which carry pigment. Their shape can be compared
with melanosomes in living birds to work out colour. Sinosauropteryx is now known
to have been ginger-brown with a striped tail, and Anchiornis had black and grey
feathers with a red crest.

## Eggs, nests and babies

All dinosaurs hatched from eggs. Dinosaur eggs were surprisingly small, never
much larger than a football, because a very large egg would need a shell too
thick for a baby to break out of.

Some dinosaurs cared for their young. Maiasaura, whose name means good mother
lizard, nested in large groups, and its nests contain babies whose bones suggest
they stayed in the nest and were fed after hatching.

## What did dinosaurs sound like?

Nobody knows for certain, because voices do not fossilise. Dinosaurs probably did
not roar like lions. Their closest living relatives, birds and crocodiles, mostly
make sounds with their mouths closed, producing deep booms, hisses and rumbles.

Parasaurolophus had a long hollow crest on its head connected to its nose
passages, and scientists have built models of it that produce a low foghorn-like
call. The roars in films are invented, usually made from the sounds of other
animals.

## How the dinosaurs died out

About sixty-six million years ago an asteroid roughly ten kilometres across
struck the Earth. The impact threw enormous amounts of dust and rock into the
atmosphere.

The dust blocked sunlight for months or years. Without sunlight, plants stopped
growing and died. Plant-eating animals starved, and then the meat-eaters that
depended on them starved too. The world also became much colder for a time.

At around the same period there were enormous volcanic eruptions, and many
scientists think these made conditions worse before and after the impact.

About three quarters of all species on Earth died out, including every dinosaur
except the birds. Small size probably helped the survivors: small animals need
less food, and birds with beaks could live on seeds and nuts, which survive in
the ground long after living plants have died.

## How we know all this

Everything we know about dinosaurs comes from fossils. A fossil forms when an
animal is buried quickly, usually in mud or sand, and minerals gradually replace
the bone over millions of years.

Scientists who study them are called palaeontologists. Besides bones, they study
trace fossils: footprints, which show how fast a dinosaur walked and whether it
travelled in a herd, eggs and nests, and even fossilised droppings, which are
called coprolites and reveal what the animal ate.

Dinosaur DNA has never been found. DNA breaks down over time and none survives
after sixty-six million years, so it is not possible to bring dinosaurs back the
way the films suggest.
